Pierian word maker

Give a Proto-Indo-European root and its meaning. The page carries it into Pierian through Early Pierian and the five periods of sound change, then ranks the results by how well they fit the language.

End a bare root with a hyphen to try the stems for its word class. A form without a hyphen is used as typed.

Word class

Only nouns for people and animals have a plural.

Each line gives the root, then optionally the meaning, the word class and the domain. Separate them with semicolons, tabs or vertical bars. The word class is noun, verb or adj; write person or animal for a noun with a plural, or thing for one without, if the meaning does not show it.

How to type PIE

Use Unicode or plain ASCII: bʰ or bh, ḱ or k', kʷ or kw, h₁ or h1, r̥ or R, ē or e:. Accents, asterisks and the root sign are ignored. A syllabic resonant between consonants is found on its own, so mrtos is read as *mr̥tós.

Bare roots end in a hyphen. Nouns then get the endings *-os -eh₂ -is -om and a root noun, adjectives get *-os -eh₂, and verbs are thematic, or athematic after a vowel or laryngeal.

How results are ranked

Every root is derived through both layers of the inherited vocabulary. The hill layer is preferred for kin, the body, herding, animals, landscape, weather and the home, and the main layer for everything else. The domain is guessed from the meaning unless you choose one.

A result scores higher when its layer suits the meaning, when its Early Pierian form has about two syllables and no three-consonant cluster, and when it reaches period 5 as a sayable word that does not sound like an older word. Results that sound like a word already in the lexicon are flagged but not marked down. The score is only a guide.

How nouns decline

Every noun is shown with its object in -ną, its locative in -ę, -vę or -yę, and its old genitive, which survives in hymns and fixed phrases. Nouns for people and animals also have a plural in -v, which takes the same endings.

Nouns that decline alike form a house, named after one of its members, such as the myääh house (‘mountain’). Three things place a noun in its house: the change to its stem before -ną, any further change before the locative, and the shape of the locative. A pattern followed by four nouns or fewer makes no house, and its nouns are orphans (auv), as is any noun whose plural breaks the plural rules.

How verbs conjugate

Every verb is shown with its four principal parts, the present, imperative, past and participle, and with the three endings that survive in hymns. The participle is also a verbal noun and an agent noun, and it declines in a house like any other noun. The forms made with auxiliaries and particles are listed below the parts.

Verbs fall into four conjugations by the way the vowel changes from one part to another, each named after a model verb: vai ‘see’, böy ‘drink’, zheem ‘come’ and viil ‘want’. The tone letters -h and -q follow from the sound the root ended in, whatever the conjugation. A vowel pattern followed by four verbs or fewer makes no conjugation, and its verbs are irregular, as is any verb whose first syllable changes, whose past has a syllable more than its imperative, or whose participle has another onset.
